## Field Note: The Black Spot That Wasn't Rot

Three summers ago, my cherry tomatoes looked perfect until they didn't.

The first sign was a dime-sized dark patch on the blossom end — the bottom, opposite the stem. Within two days it spread to a quarter. Within four, the fruit was garbage. Not the whole plant. Just the fruit. One by one, every tomato I had nurtured for eight weeks turned into a science experiment in decay.

I did what every gardening forum told me to do:
- Added calcium fertilizer ($12)
- Changed potting mix to a "tomato-specific" blend ($18)
- Sprayed foliar calcium ($9)
- Checked soil pH (fine)
- Cursed the seed company (free, but ineffective)

Nothing worked. The black spots kept coming.

**What I eventually learned:** Blossom end rot is not a calcium deficiency in the soil. It is a calcium *uptake* failure caused by irregular watering.

Tomato roots absorb calcium through water. When the soil swings from wet to dry to wet — which happens constantly in containers on hot balconies — the root system cannot maintain steady calcium transport. The fruit forms without it. The black spot is literally a hydration logistics problem.

I fixed it in one week by changing one thing: I stopped hand-watering and installed a drip system with a timer.

Not more calcium. Not better soil. Consistent moisture.

That season, after the drip system was running, every new fruit formed clean. The plants already damaged stayed damaged — calcium does not travel backward into formed tissue. But every tomato after week three was perfect.

## Deep Dive: Why Container Tomatoes Are Especially Vulnerable

In-ground tomatoes have soil buffers. Container tomatoes have whatever you give them, and they lose it fast.

**The three container-specific factors:**

1. **Soil volume is tiny**
   A 5-gallon pot holds about 0.7 cubic feet of soil. A garden tomato has access to 10+ cubic feet. Small volume = rapid moisture swings.

2. **Heat reflection bakes roots**
   Concrete balconies radiate heat upward. Soil temperature in a black plastic pot on a south-facing balcony can reach 95°F on an 80°F day. Hot roots take up water — and calcium — less efficiently.

3. **Wind strips moisture**
   Balcony wind at 5-10 mph increases evaporation by 30-50% compared to ground-level gardens. A pot that holds moisture for 24 hours in a backyard might dry in 14 hours on a balcony.

**The result:** Even if you water daily, hand-watering creates peaks and valleys. Drip irrigation creates a steady line.

## Reader Question: "Can I fix existing fruit with calcium spray?"

Short answer: no.

Calcium does not move from leaves or existing tissue into fruit that has already formed with a deficiency. The black spot is structural damage, not a surface blemish.

What calcium spray *can* do: help new fruit that is currently forming, if the root uptake issue is also fixed. But spray without consistent watering is like putting a bandage on a leak.

**The real fix priority:**
1. Install drip (or commit to exact same time, exact same volume, every single day)
2. Set a timer so you cannot forget
3. Wait for new fruit set — usually 7-14 days for cherry tomatoes, 14-21 for slicers
4. Then evaluate whether your soil actually needs calcium (most potting mixes have plenty)
