# How Big Should Your ATO Reservoir Be for Safety?

## Intent
Safety / problem-aware article for nano reef and open-top tank owners trying to balance refill convenience against flood and salinity-risk downside.

## Working title
How Big Should Your ATO Reservoir Be for Safety? A Conservative Way to Think About Convenience vs Downside

## Search intent
- Primary intent: decide how large an ATO reservoir should be in a small tank without creating unnecessary risk
- Secondary intent: understand why “bigger means less refilling” is not the whole story
- User state: wants a sane rule of thumb or decision framework without fake exact formulas

## Angle
A safety-first sizing guide that explains why reservoir size is part of the risk system, not just a convenience choice. The goal is to help readers choose a conservative downside limit instead of sleepwalking into an oversized reservoir setup.

## Outline

### Intro
- Bigger reservoirs are convenient, but convenience is not the only variable that matters.
- In a nano tank, reservoir size can determine how bad one misread sensor, siphon, or controller problem becomes.
- The safer question is not “how long can I go between refills?” It is “what is the largest mistake I am willing to let one failure make?”

### Why reservoir size is a safety issue
- a larger reservoir increases the amount of freshwater available during a bad event
- nano tanks are more sensitive to dilution and overflow consequences
- convenience can quietly overpower caution if the setup has not proven itself yet

### Factors that should guide sizing
#### 1. Tank water volume
- small systems have less margin for top-off mistakes
- what feels modest on a bigger system may be excessive on a nano tank

#### 2. Trust in the ATO setup
- new or recently changed setups deserve more conservative downside limits
- a proven system can justify more confidence, but not blind confidence

#### 3. Time away from the tank
- longer absences create pressure to size up the reservoir
- that tradeoff should be explicit, not accidental

#### 4. Failure consequences in the actual room
- floor risk
- stand/electrical risk
- downstream hassle if a problem happens while no one is around

### A conservative decision framework
- start smaller when the system is new, recently changed, or not fully trusted
- increase convenience only after the setup behaves predictably over time
- think in terms of worst-case consequence, not just refill annoyance
- remember that reservoir sizing and tubing layout work together as part of the risk profile

### Bad assumptions to avoid
- “my backup sensor makes reservoir size irrelevant”
- “I only need to worry about the sensor, not the volume available to dose”
- “if the tank is small, the reservoir problem must also be small”
- “larger always means better because I refill less often”

### When a smaller reservoir is the better choice
- the setup is still being dialed in
- the sensor area gets dirty quickly
- tubing route or mount stability still feels provisional
- the owner values downside control more than refill frequency
